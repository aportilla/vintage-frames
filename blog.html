What 512K taught me about scope

18 June 2026 · Craft · 7 min read

The original Macintosh shipped with 128 kilobytes of RAM and no hard disk. Everything an application did had to fit in the space left over after the system claimed its share, which was not much. That constraint produced a particular kind of software: programs that did one job, opened instantly, and had a manual you could finish in an afternoon.

I keep coming back to it because the constraint is gone but the lesson isn't. Nobody is short on memory now. We are short on the thing memory used to force: a decision about what the program is for. When you can add anything, "should we" stops being a technical question and becomes a taste question, and taste is much harder to delegate.

The useful move, I think, is to invent the budget you no longer have. Pick a number — screens, dependencies, settings, whatever the unit of bloat is in your domain — and treat it as fixed. The number itself barely matters. What matters is that adding now costs something, so the argument happens before the code does.

The case for the boring stack

2 May 2026 · Engineering · 5 min read

Every interesting technical decision I have regretted was interesting. Every boring one I have regretted was boring for the wrong reason — chosen out of inertia rather than judgment. The difference is worth naming, because "use boring technology" gets repeated as though inertia and judgment were the same thing.

A boring choice made well is one where you have already read the failure modes and decided you can live with them. You know how it behaves at 3am. You know which of your colleagues has debugged it before. That knowledge is the actual asset, and it is not transferable to the exciting thing no matter how good the exciting thing's documentation is.

Bitmap fonts are having a moment

27 March 2026 · Design · 4 min read

A bitmap face has no opinion about sub-pixel positioning because it cannot have one. Every stem is on the grid or it is wrong. That rigidity used to be a limitation you worked around; on a high-density display it reads as confidence, which is a strange thing for a technical constraint to become.

The catch is that they only work at their design size and integer multiples of it. Set a 16-pixel face at 21 pixels and you get mush. Any interface built on one inherits that rule, which means the interface has to know what its own scale factor is — a question most design systems never have to ask.

Reading the manual, again

11 February 2026 · Craft · 6 min read

I reread the Human Interface Guidelines most years. Not for the specifics — half of them describe hardware nobody has owned in three decades — but for the tone. It is a document written by people who believed that being consistent was a kindness to the user rather than a constraint on the designer.

The section on feedback still holds up completely. If the machine is doing something, say so. If it will take a while, say how long. If the user did something that cannot be undone, make them confirm it, and make the confirmation specific enough that reading it is actually useful.

Undo is a feature, not a safety net

30 November 2025 · Design · 3 min read

Treating undo as damage control gets you one level of it, bolted on late, that covers the operations someone remembered to wire up. Treating it as a feature gets you a model of what a change even is in your application — which turns out to be the same model you need for collaboration, for audit logs, and for explaining the app to a new user.
